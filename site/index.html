What 2.5 million serves say

01

A player's "best spot" is mostly noise

We took every player's first-serve win rate going T vs. going wide in matches through 2024, then checked it against what happened in 2025–26. If those splits were real, the dots would sit on the dashed diagonal. The best-fit line is nearly flat: about of an ATP split carries forward, and for WTA. Simply assuming "no difference" forecast 2025–26 better than the raw splits did.

That is what game theory predicts. If one spot paid better, returners would cheat toward it until it stopped paying. Walker & Wooders found pros sit at this balance in 10 Wimbledon finals. Testing T vs. wide inside each of player-court splits, come out "significant", about the 5% pure chance would flag. After correcting for testing that many players at once, not one split survives.

For coaches: don't rebuild a serve plan around a split from a handful of matches. ServeTell's numbers are shrunk by how much evidence backs them.

02

One pattern is real: body first serves lose

Across both tours, first serves aimed at the body win about fewer points per 100 than serves to the T or out wide, on both courts. It is the one target gap that shows up the same way on both tours and both courts, with hundreds of thousands of serves behind it.

For servers: keep the body serve as a surprise, not a staple. Caveat: charters log where the ball lands, so a mis-hit wide or T serve can be recorded as "body". Part of this gap is mis-hits.

03

04

Break points don't change the mix

We compared each server's mix on break points to their own mix on every other point. On break points they go to their favorite spot of the time vs. expected (ATP), and vs. (WTA). No tour-wide pressure tell. What does change is the result: servers win about fewer break points than other points, whichever way they aim.

For returners: don't guess "they'll go to their favorite" on big points. Check the individual player in the scout above, but samples there are small.

How it works

Data

The Match Charting Project: volunteer, shot-by-shot charts of pro matches ( ATP, WTA, ). We parsed every point into serve attempts. For each one we know first or second serve, the target (wide, body, T), the court (deuce or ad, worked out from the score), break-point status, surface, both players, and who won the point.

Model

A penalized logistic regression predicts the chance the server wins the point. It combines a tour baseline (serve × court × target × surface) with each server's and each returner's overall strength, their strength by serve, and their target-specific quirks. The penalty pulls every player effect toward zero unless the data insists, the same as a random-effects model. Penalties were tuned on 2024 matches. Target-specific quirks needed the heaviest penalty (λ = ), which is finding 01 again.

Validation

We trained on matches through 2024 and scored on ATP and WTA matches from 2025–26 that the model never saw.

Point outcomes are close to coin flips, so log-loss gains look small. They are steady across tours and splits. Hold % comes from the standard game formula applied to the forecast serve-point win rate.

Limitations

  • Charted matches lean toward big events and well-known players. Lower-ranked players have fewer matches, so their numbers are pulled harder toward the tour norm.
  • Targets are recorded where the ball lands, not where it was aimed.
  • The switching test shuffles serves within each match. A server who changes plan mid-match (say, attacking one weakness for a set) shows up as a "repeater", so repeat counts mix habit with in-match adjustment.
  • The direction tests are observational. Players pick targets based on things we can't see (the returner's position, wind, fatigue).
  • Our "two first serves" check assumes a first serve keeps its usual accuracy when hit as a second serve. Under pressure it likely won't, so the real gain is even smaller.
